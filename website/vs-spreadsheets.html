GoWarm Work · The honest comparison

The spreadsheet is not wrong. It is just out of arguments.

Nearly every company under 500 people runs projects and daily work out of a shared sheet, and for a long time that is the correct decision. This page is about the point where it stops being correct, what specifically breaks, and what has to replace each broken part.

When does a shared spreadsheet stop working for project tracking?

Not at a headcount, but at four conditions: more than one person writes to the same record in the same week, the record has to outlive somebody's memory, a number from it is used to make a decision, or you cannot tell from the sheet whether work is happening at all. A spreadsheet structurally cannot tell absence from silence, enforce a sequence, require evidence, aggregate free text, keep a ledger, or remember what a date used to be.

First, what the sheet is genuinely good at.

Any comparison that opens by calling spreadsheets primitive is not worth reading. The sheet won for reasons, and they are good ones.

It is free. Everyone already knows how to use it, so there is no adoption problem and no training. It takes about four minutes to set up and it can be reshaped in the middle of a project without asking anyone. It imposes no opinion about how you work, which means it never gets in the way. For one project, one owner and a horizon under a month, a sheet is the right tool and reaching for software instead is an act of procrastination.

The question is never whether the spreadsheet is good. It is whether the thing you now need is something a spreadsheet can structurally do.

Six things a sheet cannot do, however well you build it.

Not gaps in your formula skills. Each of these is a property the format does not have, so no amount of conditional formatting recovers it.

1. Tell absence from silence

A blank cell might mean a holiday, a quiet day, or that someone stopped filling it in six weeks ago. The sheet cannot distinguish the three because it has no working calendar underneath it, which means no number derived from it can be trusted.

What replaces it: a schedule and a holiday calendar per person, so a missing day is either expected or a real absence — and compliance becomes days logged divided by working days.

2. Enforce a sequence

Nothing stops row 14 being marked done before row 9. Dependencies exist in the head of whoever built the sheet, and when that person is on leave the sequence silently stops existing at all.

What replaces it: task dependencies validated for circularity when they are created, and stage gates that hold the next stage shut until the current one is genuinely met.

3. Require evidence

In a sheet, "done" means whatever the person typing it believes. That is an assertion rather than a record, and six months later there is no way to tell a completed step from an optimistic one.

What replaces it: a task closes with something attached — a file, a link, a note that cannot be quietly rewritten.

4. Aggregate free text

"Client call", "Call with client" and "customer discussion" are three lines in every summary you build. The sheet can be read but it cannot be counted, so it never answers a question about the whole team.

What replaces it: a controlled activity vocabulary, with an "Other" escape hatch that never blocks anyone and feeds a queue the manager promotes or merges from.

5. Keep a ledger

Any figure in a sheet can be edited in place, so a cost record is a current opinion rather than a history. Correct a mistake and the mistake is gone; fold a variation into the original quantities and the project looks on budget while the reason it grew disappears.

What replaces it: an append-only spend ledger of increments, rates snapshotted on every entry, corrections booked as reversals pointing at the original, and approved variations kept separate from the bill.

6. Remember what a date used to be

Change a date in a sheet and the previous one is gone. Do that four times over a project and it was never late, because there is nothing left to have been late against.

What replaces it: a baseline frozen when the plan is committed, so drift is a measured difference rather than a monthly renegotiation.

The clearest example: what an empty cell means.

This one is worth drawing, because it is the failure people find hardest to see. It looks like a small data-quality issue and it is actually the reason the whole sheet cannot support a decision.

The fix is not stricter data entry. It is putting a working calendar behind the record so the blank has only two possible meanings instead of three.

What has to survive the move.

Most replacements fail because they are heavier than the sheet. The properties that made the spreadsheet win have to be preserved, not traded away.

The floor stays low

One line a day, written where the person already is, chosen from a list. If the replacement asks for more effort than the sheet did, a shadow spreadsheet appears within three months and you have two systems instead of one.

Nothing generates work

No scheduler creates rows for anyone, so there is no backlog to fall behind on. A sheet never nagged you and neither does this.

You can still change your mind

The activity list is meant to be edited in the first month rather than perfected before you start, and the manager curates it from what people actually typed.

Setup stays small

Departments, working schedules, a holiday calendar and a first activity list. Most teams are running within a week, not a quarter.

The plan comes with you

A project checklist can be imported rather than retyped, and the baseline freezes when you commit the plan — so an in-flight project can move across and still be measured from there.

The messaging group is not ignored

Conversations your teams already use can be attached to the project they belong to, on channels your organisation chooses, so decisions stop scrolling away.

When you should keep the spreadsheet.

One project, one owner, a horizon under a month, and no decision riding on a number the sheet produces. In that situation software is overhead and the sheet is the correct answer. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something.

The four conditions that flip it: more than one person writes to the same record in the same week; the record has to outlive somebody's memory; a number from it is used to make a decision; or you cannot tell, from the sheet alone, whether work is happening at all. Any one of those is enough. Most companies cross all four within a year of each other and only notice the last one.

Questions we get asked

Can we import the sheet?

A project checklist can be imported rather than retyped. What cannot come across is history the sheet never captured, which is usually most of it — old dates, who changed what, what proved a step was done.

Will people just keep using the spreadsheet?

Only if the replacement asks for more than the sheet did. A shadow sheet appearing a few months in is a reliable sign the tool was too heavy, which is why the bar here is one line a day.

We use the sheet plus a WhatsApp group.

That is the real system in most companies this size. An administrator can attach the channels your organisation chooses to the project they belong to, so decisions land against the work instead of scrolling away.

Our sheet has formulas nobody else understands.

That is its own argument. A record that only one person can maintain is a single point of failure, and it is usually the person you can least afford to have doing data entry.
